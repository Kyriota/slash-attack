Scratched Lenses, Shifted Depth: Passive Camera-Side Optical Attacks

Qinlin He, Zeming Zhuang, Yongji Wu, Lan Zhang, Xiaoyong (Brian) Yuan

Clemson University

NDSS Symposium 2027

Paper Code (coming soon) BibTeX

A few small scratches on a camera's protective cover can make AI depth-perception models misjudge how far away a car is. In our strongest outdoor test case, a car about 20 m away was estimated at roughly 26 m. The scratch stays dormant: it only acts when a headlight or a glint of sunlight shines through it. We call this attack SLASH (Scratch-induced Lens Adversarial Streak Hijacking).

The Attack in Four Steps

  1. 1. Design

    Offline, the attacker computes where to put a couple of straight scratches so their streaks will mislead the depth model.

  2. 2. Scratch

    During a brief moment of unattended access, such as parking or servicing, the attacker scratches the lens or its protective cover.

  3. 3. Walk away

    The scratch stays in place. The attacker never touches the car, its software, or the model again.

  4. 4. The scene triggers it

    When headlights or sunlight glints on another car hit the scratch, a streak appears right next to that car and shifts its predicted distance.

Why It Matters

Most physical attacks on cameras change what the camera sees: a patch on a stop sign, a projector, a laser. A few recent attacks instead modify the camera, with a printed sticker or an extra optical element on the lens, but those alter every single frame. Among these attack classes, SLASH is the only one that sits on the camera, needs nobody present afterward, lasts, and only acts when the scene triggers it. A scratch also has a built-in excuse: lenses get scratched all the time, and a streak around a bright light looks like flare from a worn lens, not like an attack.

Why does a streak fool a depth model? Guessing distance from a single photo is an ill-posed problem, so depth models lean on visual cues: edges, highlights, shading, and where an object meets the road. A bright streak lands exactly on those cues, right next to the car whose light created it. The model has no way to know the streak came from the lens rather than the scene, so it treats the streak as real evidence about the car's shape and distance.

How SLASH Works

The attacker cannot paint pixels; they can only choose where to scratch. SLASH therefore designs the attack in terms of the physical scratch and lets optics decide what the image looks like.

  1. Optics decides where the streak goes

    Each straight scratch is described by just two numbers: its angle and its position. A simple lens model then predicts the streak's position, direction, and length in the image for any light source in the scene.

  2. A lightweight renderer draws it

    Instead of an expensive physics simulation, SLASH paints a soft, light-like streak that brightens the image the way real scattered light does. Depth models react mainly to the streak's position, direction, size, and contrast, so fine photometric detail is not needed.

  3. One scratch for the whole scene

    A scratch cannot be changed after it is made, so SLASH searches for a single scratch pattern that works across every frame of a scene as the target car moves. The search only needs the model's outputs, not its gradients.

Results

We attacked three depth-estimation models and three 3D object detectors. The attacker picks a direction: make the target car look closer or farther.

32% closer than the model's normal estimate (driving data, simulated scratches)
36% farther than the model's normal estimate (driving data, simulated scratches)
7.0 m distance shift at night with real scratched covers (4.7 m by day)
2 m reported by a stereo camera for a surface 21 m away, with only one of its two lenses scratched

On driving data

We cannot scratch the cameras that recorded the public nuScenes driving dataset, so for these tests we add the streaks with our renderer. The same scratch pattern shifts the target car's predicted depth across a whole sequence of frames, by day and at night. Random scratches shift it less than optimized ones in every setting, so the effect comes from the optimized placement, not from scratches in general. 3D detectors are harder to push (up to about 9%), but the attack still moves their distance estimates in the chosen direction.

A scratch has to be made before the attacker knows which car it will meet. Tested on scenes and cars never used to design it (at a similar distance and angle), the scratch keeps more than half of its effect in 8 of 12 settings, and up to about 90%.

On real cameras

We engraved patterns optimized on nuScenes onto acrylic and glass covers, without re-tuning them for our test site, and filmed a real SUV with a clean camera and a scratched camera stacked together. The scratched camera shifted the car's estimated distance by about 4.7 m by day and 7.0 m at night, the same meter-level range reported by prior physical attacks on depth that modify the scene or add optics to the lens. With the car's lights switched off, the same scratched cover changed the estimate by less than 2%, so the scratch really does stay dormant until triggered. The attack even fooled a stereo camera, whose second, clean lens was not enough to catch it.

Can It Be Defended?

We tested three kinds of defense. None of them removes the threat.

  • Flare removal. Off-the-shelf flare-removal networks barely dent the nighttime attack (a 23% shift drops only to about 20%), and one of them also erases real headlights and lamps.
  • Image enhancement. Generic enhancement that a driving system might already use leaves the attack in place and can even make it stronger.
  • Adversarial training. A hardened model is bypassed by simply re-optimizing the scratch against it.

An effective defense likely needs to check the camera's optical path itself, not just the images or depth it produces.

FAQ

Is my car affected?

We did not test any commercial vehicle. Our study targets open research models for depth estimation and 3D detection, on public driving data and on our own camera rig with an exposed protective cover. Cameras behind a curved windshield are untested, and systems that fuse cameras with LiDAR or radar may be affected differently. The broader finding is that camera-based depth perception treats lens artifacts as real scene content.

Wouldn't someone notice the scratch?

The scratches are small and easy to miss without a close look at the right angle. When they do produce a streak, it looks like flare from an ordinary worn or dirty lens. Someone who notices it would most likely blame wear rather than suspect an attack.

What does the attacker need?

A short window of unattended access to the camera, comparable to parking or a service visit, and something to scratch with. To design the pattern, they need rough camera specifications (focal length, aperture, sensor size) and a model of the same kind to test against offline. They never touch the vehicle's electronics, software, or model.

Does it need a bright light?

Yes. Headlights at night are the strongest trigger. In daytime, sunlight reflecting off the target car's body also works. Without a compact bright source, the scratch has almost no effect.

Does one scratch work on any car, anywhere?

Not anywhere. A scratch pattern works best for targets at a similar distance and angle to the ones it was designed for. Within that range it carries over to new roads and new cars.

Why depth, and not just object detection?

Decisions such as braking depend on how far away other cars are. In our nighttime driving-data tests, the 3D detectors still found the target car in more than 99% of cases but placed it at the wrong distance, so later modules receive an input that looks valid and is wrong.

Abstract

Physical adversarial attacks on vision systems are typically studied via scene manipulation, such as adversarial patches or projections, where the adversary controls what the camera observes at capture time. A smaller body of work has examined camera-side manipulations, such as camera stickers and auxiliary optics, which introduce consistent artifacts in the captured image. However, these approaches treat the attack as an image-space perturbation produced by designed transmissive patterns, without explicitly modeling how physical imperfections interact with scene-dependent lighting and optics. In contrast, we identify a distinct and underexplored threat: passive lens-side damage that is persistent yet trigger-conditioned, producing scene-dependent optical artifacts that bias geometric inference.

We instantiate this threat through Scratch-induced Lens Adversarial Streak Hijacking (SLASH), a physical-world attack caused by small scratches on a camera lens or protective cover. These scratches interact with bright point-like light sources and specular reflections to generate structured optical artifacts that systematically distort depth cues. Crucially, the perturbation is fixed in the optical path but conditionally triggered by the scene, making it both persistent and selective. To capture this behavior, we formulate the attack in optical space rather than pixel space, modeling the scratch pattern as a trigger-conditioned optical channel, and using the same scratch pattern across diverse viewing conditions. We evaluate SLASH in both digital and real-world settings on monocular depth estimation and monocular 3D object detection. On the nuScenes dataset, SLASH causes monocular depth models to estimate target vehicles as up to 32% closer to the camera or 36% farther away, with consistent effects extending to monocular 3D object detection. Physical experiments with both acrylic and glass covers confirm that the attack produces substantial depth errors on real camera inputs. These findings reveal a new attack surface in which benign-looking hardware imperfections act as latent, scene-triggered adversarial mechanisms, challenging existing assumptions about physical robustness and highlighting the need to account for optical-path perturbations in secure vision system design.
