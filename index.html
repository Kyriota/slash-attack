SLASH Scratched Lenses, Shifted Depth: Passive Camera-Side Optical Attacks

Qinlin He, Zeming Zhuang, Yongji Wu, Lan Zhang, Xiaoyong (Brian) Yuan

Clemson University

Paper (arXiv) Code (coming soon) BibTeX

TL;DR A few small scratches on a camera's protective cover can make depth-perception models misjudge how far away a car is. The scratch stays dormant until a headlight or a glint of sunlight shines through it.

Abstract

Physical adversarial attacks on vision systems are typically studied via scene manipulation, such as adversarial patches or projections, where the adversary controls what the camera observes at capture time. A smaller body of work has examined camera-side manipulations, such as camera stickers and auxiliary optics, which introduce consistent artifacts in the captured image. However, these approaches treat the attack as an image-space perturbation produced by designed transmissive patterns, without explicitly modeling how physical imperfections interact with scene-dependent lighting and optics. In contrast, we identify a distinct and underexplored threat: passive lens-side damage that is persistent yet trigger-conditioned, producing scene-dependent optical artifacts that bias geometric inference.

We instantiate this threat through Scratch-induced Lens Adversarial Streak Hijacking (SLASH), a physical-world attack caused by small scratches on a camera lens or protective cover. These scratches interact with bright point-like light sources and specular reflections to generate structured optical artifacts that systematically distort depth cues. Crucially, the perturbation is fixed in the optical path but conditionally triggered by the scene, making it both persistent and selective. To capture this behavior, we formulate the attack in optical space rather than pixel space, modeling the scratch pattern as a trigger-conditioned optical channel, and using the same scratch pattern across diverse viewing conditions. We evaluate SLASH in both digital and real-world settings on monocular depth estimation and monocular 3D object detection. On the nuScenes dataset, SLASH causes monocular depth models to estimate target vehicles as up to 32% closer to the camera or 36% farther away, with consistent effects extending to monocular 3D object detection. Physical experiments with both acrylic and glass covers confirm that the attack produces substantial depth errors on real camera inputs. These findings reveal a new attack surface in which benign-looking hardware imperfections act as latent, scene-triggered adversarial mechanisms, challenging existing assumptions about physical robustness and highlighting the need to account for optical-path perturbations in secure vision system design.

Threat Model

The attacker needs only a brief window of unattended physical access to the camera. After that, the scratch is fixed and the attacker is absent.

  1. STEP 1

    Design

    Offline, the attacker computes where to place a couple of straight scratches so their streaks will mislead the depth model.

  2. STEP 2

    Deploy

    During a window such as parking or servicing, the attacker scratches the lens or its protective cover.

  3. STEP 3

    Leave

    The scratch stays in place. The attacker never touches the vehicle, its software, or the model again.

  4. STEP 4

    Scene trigger

    When headlights or sunlight reflected from another car hit the scratch, a streak appears next to that car and shifts its predicted distance.

Comparison with prior physical attacks

Most physical attacks change what the camera sees, through a patch in the scene, a projector, or a laser. A few recent attacks modify the camera with a printed sticker or an extra optical element, but those alter every frame. Among these attack classes, SLASH is the only one that is camera-side, passive after deployment, persistent, and triggered by the scene. A scratched lens is also common wear, and its streak resembles ordinary lens flare.

Method Overview

The attacker cannot choose pixels, only where to scratch. SLASH therefore optimizes the physical scratch parameters and lets the camera optics determine how the streak appears in the image.

  1. Geometry-constrained scratch-to-streak mapping

    Each straight scratch is described by two numbers, its angle and its offset. A thin-lens model predicts where the streak appears, which direction it follows, and how long it is for every light source in the scene.

  2. Streak appearance synthesis

    A lightweight renderer draws a soft, light-like streak that brightens the image as scattered light would. Depth models respond mainly to the streak's position, direction, size, and contrast, so a full physics simulation is unnecessary.

  3. Scene-specific optimization

    A scratch cannot be changed after deployment, so SLASH searches for one scratch pattern that remains effective across every frame of a scene as the target vehicle moves. The search uses only model outputs, not gradients.

Why a streak affects depth estimation

Estimating distance from a single image is underconstrained, so depth models rely on visual cues such as edges, highlights, shading, and where an object meets the road. A streak lands on exactly these cues, next to the vehicle whose light produced it. The model cannot tell that the streak comes from the lens rather than the scene, so it treats the streak as evidence about the vehicle's shape and distance.

Results

We attack three monocular depth estimators (MonoDepth2, md4all, DCPI-Depth) and three monocular 3D detectors (FCOS3D, PGD, OVM3D). The attacker chooses a direction: make the target vehicle appear closer or farther.

32% closer than the model's clean estimate (nuScenes, rendered streaks)
36% farther than the model's clean estimate (nuScenes, rendered streaks)
7.0 m depth shift at night with real scratched covers (4.7 m by day)
2 m reported by a stereo camera for a surface 21 m away, with one lens scratched

Digital attacks on nuScenes

The streaks are rendered onto nuScenes frames, since the cameras that recorded the dataset cannot be scratched. One scratch pattern shifts the target's predicted depth across a whole frame sequence, by day and at night. Random scratches shift it less than optimized ones in every setting. 3D detectors are harder to push (up to about 9%), but their distance estimates still move in the chosen direction.

Unseen scenes and vehicles

A scratch must be made before the attacker knows which vehicle it will meet. On scenes and vehicles not used for optimization, at a similar distance and angle, the scratch retains more than half of its effect in 8 of 12 settings, and up to about 90%.

Physical experiments

Patterns optimized on nuScenes were engraved onto acrylic and glass covers without re-tuning for the test site. The scratched camera shifted the vehicle's estimated distance by about 4.7 m by day and 7.0 m at night, within the meter-level range reported by prior physical attacks on depth estimation. With the vehicle's lights switched off, the same cover changed the estimate by less than 2%.

Stereo camera

With only one of its two lenses scratched, a commercial stereo camera reported a surface about 2 m away where the true surface was 21 m away. The clean second view did not prevent the attack.

Countermeasures

We evaluate three defense directions. None of them neutralizes SLASH in our evaluation.

Flare removal

Off-the-shelf flare-removal networks barely reduce the nighttime attack (a 23% shift drops only to about 20%), and one of them also removes real headlights and lamps.

Image enhancement

Generic enhancement that a driving system might already apply retains the attack effect and can amplify it.

Adversarial training

A hardened model is bypassed by re-optimizing the scratch against it.

An effective defense likely needs to inspect the optical path itself, not only the images or depth it produces.
